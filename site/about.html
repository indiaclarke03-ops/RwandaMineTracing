What this tool is

Ground Control is an open investigation tool about conflict minerals: metals mined in places where armed groups control or tax the mines, so that buying them can fund violence. It answers four questions, in order:

  1. Control. Which armed group controls, taxes or is present at which mining site, since when, and according to whom?
  2. Supply chain. Where do the minerals go next: through which trading centres, border crossings, exporters, ports, traders and smelters?
  3. Impacts. What happens along the way: money to armed groups, forced and child labour, deaths, and exports that don't match production.
  4. Response. What have governments and companies done about it, such as sanctions, peace deals, due-diligence laws and certification suspensions, and did the things those actions were meant to change actually change?

Why it matters: the people who dig

Most coltan, tin ore and gold in eastern Congo is dug by hand. In armed-group-held areas, UN investigators, Amnesty International and Human Rights Watch have documented summary killings of miners, forced labour in underground pits, children in the pits, ransoms, illegal taxes and deadly landslides. Abuses are not committed only by M23: the same sources document abuses by the Congolese army, allied Wazalendo militias, CODECO and others. The site records who is alleged to have done what, with the evidence label, and never names victims.

UN totals for sexual violence, child recruitment and displacement cover eastern Congo as a whole, not only mines. The site says so wherever they appear.

How much we rely on these mines

On US Geological Survey figures, Congo and Rwanda account for about 68% of the world's mined tantalum, about 11% of its tin and under 1% of its gold. Rwanda's tantalum figure is estimated from its exports, so it already includes ore smuggled from Congo. Tantalum goes mainly into capacitors in electronics and into jet-engine alloys, and less than 1% is recycled. Per-device amounts are shown only where a credible source measured them.

The first case: Rwanda and eastern DR Congo

Eastern Democratic Republic of the Congo produces a large share of the world's tantalum (from the ore coltan, used in capacitors in phones and laptops), as well as tin, tungsten and gold. In April 2024 the M23 armed group took the Rubaya mining area in North Kivu. UN investigators say M23 taxes production there and controls the routes into Rwanda, where Congolese minerals are mixed with Rwandan output and exported as Rwandan. The US and the UN Group of Experts say Rwanda's army backs M23. Rwanda denies this, and its denials are shown next to those claims.

Later cases will reuse the same structure: rare earths in Myanmar, gold in the Sahel and Sudan, and gold in Colombia and Venezuela.

How the evidence is graded

Control in eastern Congo is contested and changes quickly. Most claims about who profits are allegations, not court findings. So every claim on this site carries one of four labels, shown with an icon and a line style as well as a colour:

LabelMeansExample
◆DesignatedNamed in a government sanctions or enforcement action (US OFAC, EU, UN)OFAC designated Gasabo Gold Refinery in June 2026
■DocumentedSet out with evidence by the UN Group of Experts or an investigative NGOThe UN says M23 has held Rubaya since 30 April 2024
●ReportedMedia reportingDeath tolls from the 2026 Rubaya landslides
△InferredOur own analysis. It always says how it was worked out.Whether a sanction changed an indicator

We never move a claim up a level: an NGO allegation stays "documented" even when it is widely repeated. Where a named party has denied a claim, the denial appears beside it.

How the data is gathered

Rules every record follows

  • Every record has a source. The build fails if any row lacks one. Each source was read in full before it was cited; search results alone are never used.
  • Every claim is dated with when it was true and when it was published.
  • Text is paraphrased. We link to reports rather than copying them.

Sources

  • Control: UN Group of Experts on the DRC reports, Amnesty International and Global Witness for the key sites. The map also shows IPIS's 3,469 surveyed artisanal mining sites, with armed presence at each researcher visit from 2009 to 2026. IPIS records who was present at a visit, not who controls a site today, and does not record M23.
  • Supply chain: Sayari corporate and trade records, UN Comtrade, Global Witness, ITSCI.
  • Responses: US Treasury and State Department releases, the EU Official Journal, peace-agreement texts and ITSCI statements.

Company matching (Sayari)

A company in a report is linked to a Sayari record only after checking country, identifiers, address and relationships, not on name alone. Every match stores the reason. Weak matches are labelled low-confidence, as are "possibly-same-as" risk flags.

Each company links to Sayari Graph so you can check it yourself. Sayari is strongest where a government has already sanctioned someone. It has no ownership records for most private Rwandan and Congolese companies.

Trade records

Customs data shows who shipped what to whom, not where the mineral was mined. A coltan shipment from Rwanda is not, by itself, evidence of smuggling.

The mineral balance

If a country exports more of a mineral than it can plausibly mine, the difference has to come from somewhere. Rwanda publishes no mine-level output, and USGS's "production" figures for Rwandan coltan are mostly reported exports, so a simple "exports minus production" sum shows no gap by construction. Congo's national figures have the same problem.

We use three checks instead:

  1. Geology. In this region's mixed deposits, coltan is about a quarter of combined coltan and tin-ore output (Germany's geological survey, cited by the UN Group of Experts). So Rwanda's tin-ore exports predict its coltan exports. They matched from 2018 to 2022; from 2023 coltan pulled ahead. For 2025 we freeze the tin-ore baseline at its 2022–24 level, because that year's tin-ore surge itself looks non-domestic.
  2. Province-level loss. We compare Rwanda's excess with the fall in official North and South Kivu output, from Congo's mining ministry tables. A national "missing output" does not exist: the signal appears only by province.
  3. Destinations. We use importers' reported figures and Sayari customs records to see which destinations grew.

To estimate how much of the excess could come from armed-group-held areas, we take published volumes (such as the UN's 120–150 t a month for Rubaya), assume 50, 75 or 100% reached Rwanda, and divide by the excess. Those rates are our judgement and no source states the resulting shares. The page lists what would disprove them, next to Rwanda's own explanations.

Tracing unsanctioned companies

Sayari traces owners well where a government has already sanctioned someone, but it has no Rwandan company registry. For unsanctioned exporters and traders we combine UN and NGO reports, customs records and open registries in Switzerland, Cyprus, the UK and Uganda, then grade the evidence of an armed-group link:

GradeMeans
StrongA UN report plus a second organisation, which may share sources
ModerateOne organisation's evidence, or a buyer alleged to have bought from a named exporter
WeakConcerns raised, such as ITSCI incidents, with no source saying it bought from an armed-group area
None foundCustoms rows or shared buyers only

Customs and registry records add structure, but every armed-group link still rests on UN or NGO evidence. Shared buyers and addresses are shown as network signals and never count as a path to an armed group. Grades are allegations, not findings of knowledge or intent.

Smelters and brands

Brand links come from US Conflict Minerals Reports and similar filings, where companies list smelters their suppliers named. Most say a smelter "may" be in the supply chain. A listing is not evidence that a company received conflict minerals, and no filing says one did. "Conformant" under the RMI audit scheme means a smelter's due-diligence system passed audit, not that its ore is clean. RMI paused new China assessments in August 2026, so six statuses are frozen.

Which mines count as controlled

The map separates four kinds of evidence. Armed-group-held and contested sites are places a dated UN, NGO, government or media source covers, matched by site name or, as our inference, by a small radius around a named town. Armed group at last visit comes from IPIS researchers, who record who was present that day. Everything else is unknown at that date, not free of armed groups. Wazalendo militias count as armed groups even when allied to the army; the Congolese army alone does not.

Official statistics and company filings are labelled "documented", though they are not UN or NGO evidence: they show what was reported, not that it is true.

How "Check a company" finds paths

We join every sourced link in the dataset into one network: ownership and officer ties, sanctions findings, trade records, mineral flows and site control. For the company you pick, we find the shortest route to each armed group. When two routes are the same length, we prefer stronger evidence and current control over past control. Every step shows its evidence label and source.

A path shows that sourced connections exist. It does not show that a company knew, or that a particular shipment came from a conflict mine. Not appearing in the dataset is not a clearance.

What this tool cannot tell you

  • Who physically controls a mine today. Control changes faster than reports are published, and the control dates shown are the latest we could source.
  • The origin of the mineral in any single shipment.
  • Whether a particular buyer knew.
  • Who owns most private Rwandan and Congolese companies; registry data is not available.
  • What we have not yet checked. The What we don't know page lists every known gap.

Corrections and updates

When checking a claim against its primary source changes it, the correction is logged in the repository's corrections file. One example: a widely repeated "150 tonnes in 2024" turned out, in the UN report, to be 150 tonnes a month.

New sanctions rounds, UN reports and control changes are added after they are read and checked. Nothing is published automatically. To report an error, open an issue on GitHub.
